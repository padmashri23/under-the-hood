Open Weights vs Open Source AI: The Technical and Licensing Differences That Matter

Downloading an AI model is easy. Knowing what you're allowed to do with it, and what you still can't see, is the hard part.

Researched & written October 2026 · ~12 min read · Sources cited throughout

Picture a small team building a customer support chatbot. They find a free AI model online, Alibaba's Qwen2.5-3B, download it, and train it further on a few thousand of their old support tickets so it learns their product. It works surprisingly well. The demo goes great, and a launch date is set.

A week before launch, someone finally reads the license. This version of Qwen is licensed for non-commercial purposes only (license). Its slightly bigger sibling, Qwen2.5-7B, uses Apache 2.0, a standard license that allows business use (model card). The biggest, Qwen2.5-72B, uses a third license, which, among other rules, makes products with more than 100 million monthly users ask permission first (license). Same family, same release day, three different rule books.

Nothing stopped the download. That's the whole problem in one story. Being able to download an AI model and being allowed to build a business on it are two separate questions, and "open" gets used for both.

In 60 seconds
  • Open weights means you can download the finished model. It says nothing about what you're allowed to do with it, or how it was made.
  • Open source AI, by the Open Source Initiative's definition, also shares the code used to build the model and a detailed description of its training data.
  • The rules change from model to model, even within one company.
  • Running a model yourself keeps your data in-house, but makes security your job.

What you actually get when a model is "released"

Think of an AI model as a cake. The weights are the finished cake: billions of numbers the model learned during training, packed into files you can download. With the cake you can serve it, slice it, or add your own frosting, which in AI terms means running the model and fine-tuning it on your own data.

What the cake doesn't come with is the recipe. That would be the training code (the instructions used to build the model), the training data (the ingredients), and the tests used to check it. Then there's the license: the rules about whether you're allowed to sell slices.

Different companies put different things in the box. OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b, released in August 2025, comes with its weights and the code to run it, but its training data gets only a short description (model card). Ai2's Olmo 3, from November 2025, also publishes its training data, training code, test tools and snapshots saved partway through training (Ai2). Both are useful. Only one lets you check how it was made.

What "open source" means, according to the OSI

There is a widely recognized answer. The Open Source Initiative (OSI), the nonprofit that maintains the definition of open source software, published the Open Source AI Definition 1.0 in October 2024. It says a truly open source AI must give everyone four freedoms: to use it for any purpose without asking permission, to study how it works, to change it, and to share it, changed or not.

To make those freedoms real, you need three things: the weights, the complete code used to train and run the model, and a description of the training data detailed enough that a skilled person can build a substantially equivalent system. That doesn't mean every document has to be published. Private data, like personal information, can be described instead of shared. But weights on their own don't qualify.

When the OSI tested real models against its draft, EleutherAI's Pythia and Ai2's OLMo passed, while Meta's Llama 2 and Microsoft's Phi-2 did not (OSI FAQ). Outside the OSI, companies use these words loosely. So when someone calls a model open, ask which parts.

What you can do with just the weights

Quite a lot. Free tools like Ollama and llama.cpp let you run a model on your own computer, with no internet connection needed once it's downloaded. A technique called quantization shrinks models by storing each number less precisely, a bit like compressing a photo: smaller file, slightly lower quality. That's how OpenAI's gpt-oss-20b runs on a machine with 16 GB of memory (model card). Customizing a model is within reach, too: a method called QLoRA fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB graphics card.

What you can't do is look inside the kitchen. Meta's Llama 3.1 model card says it learned from ~15 trillion tokens of data from publicly available sources (model card). (Tokens are small chunks of text.) Meta's technical report adds a rough breakdown by topic, but the data itself isn't released (Llama 3 paper). You can't check whether a test the model aces was in its training data, like a student who saw the exam in advance. And when it gets something wrong, you can't trace the mistake back to where it came from.

The license decides what you're allowed to do

Some models use standard software licenses. Apache 2.0 and MIT both let you use, change, share and sell the model, as long as you keep the original copyright notices. Apache 2.0 also includes a patent license from the people who contributed to it. Gemma 4, gpt-oss, Phi-4, Qwen3 and IBM's Granite 4.0 all use one of these two.

Others use a company's own license, and these come with strings attached. Meta's Llama 3.1 license allows business use, but companies whose products had more than 700 million monthly users on its release date must ask Meta first. If you ship it in a product, you also have to display "Built with Llama," and every user must follow Meta's acceptable use policy (license). Llama 4's models all understand images, and for those, Meta's policy doesn't grant rights to people or companies based in the EU, though anyone can still use products built with them (policy). Google's Gemma 3 terms carry their banned uses into any modified version, and Google reserves the right to restrict (remotely or otherwise) usage it believes breaks the rules (terms). Mistral's Devstral 2 coding model isn't licensed to companies earning more than $20 million a month (license).

The rules also change from one release to the next. Gemma 4 moved to Apache 2.0 in April 2026 (Google). Mistral Large 3 did the same after Large 2 was research-only (Mistral; license). Meta's Muse Glimmer 30B, released in August 2026, is Apache 2.0 too, unlike every Llama model before it (model card). So never guess a license from the company name.

How to check a license in two minutes
  1. Open the model's page on Hugging Face and find the license tag near the top.
  2. Open the LICENSE file and search it for "commercial," "monthly active users" and "revenue."
  3. Look for a separate usage policy, and read that too.
  4. Save a copy, and note the exact model version you checked.
Not legal advice

The quotes come straight from the original documents, but how a rule applies to your business depends on your situation and where you operate. For anything important, ask a lawyer.

Running it yourself: more control, more responsibility

A big reason companies like downloadable models is privacy. When the model runs on your own computers or cloud account, your customers' messages never go to an outside AI company. For hospitals or banks, that can settle the decision.

But control isn't the same as safety. Your chat logs still hold sensitive data, and your servers still need locking down. Even model files can be risky: Hugging Face warns that a widely used file format can run hidden code when it's opened, which is why the safer "safetensors" format exists (Hugging Face). Once you run a model yourself, updates, security fixes and monitoring are all your job.

And seeing a model's weights doesn't make it trustworthy. Open models can make things up (AI people call this "hallucinating"), just like closed ones. Openness makes it possible to check a model. It doesn't do the checking for you.

So which kind should you choose?

Openness isn't automatically better. A paid AI service from a company like OpenAI or Google is the easiest place to start and often among the most capable, but you depend on their prices and rules, and your data goes to them. Open-weight models give you control and privacy in exchange for running your own hardware. Fully open models add the ability to inspect and rebuild, from a much smaller selection.

Back to the chatbot

For the team in our story, the fix was simple because they caught it early. Qwen2.5-7B and the newer Qwen3-4B both use Apache 2.0 (Qwen). They had to redo the training on the new model, which cost them an afternoon. Finding out after launch would have cost far more.

So "Is this model open?" turns out to be too vague a question. Ask what parts you actually get, what the license allows for this exact version, what you still can't see, and whether you can run it safely. If a model passes all four for your project, it's the right one, whether you download it or rent it.
